I — The assets

Two products, one player, built four years apart

Neither was built to be sold. That is the first thing worth paying for: every decision in both was made by someone who had to sit down at a table on Friday and use it.

Asset one

Two modes, seven tabs, and one real idea: what you need at the table is not what you need between sessions. PLAY is Combat, Grimoire, Roleplay. PREP is Character, Grimoire, Persona, Academy. The Grimoire collapses spells, class features and racial abilities into one list, because no player has ever thought “is this a spell or a feature?” — they think “what can my character do?”

IV — The critique

Three things I would say out loud in the first meeting

One

You wrote the differentiator down instead of building it

The acceptance test in THE_ONE_WEIRD_THING.md is perfect: “a player who has never seen The Codex should describe it to a friend by mentioning the spell slots are runes that burn to ash.” It scored itself 10/10 on differentiation and then never shipped. Meanwhile the roadmap grew Waves 1–3 of secondary signature elements. Specification is the cheapest form of progress and the most convincing kind of stall.

Two

The two assets have never met

The Codex knows everything about one character and forgets the campaign. The Vault remembers the campaign and knows nothing about the character. They were built by the same hand for the same table and there is not one line of integration between them. That gap is not a defect — it is the product, and nobody has built it.

Three

The best work is buried behind the least differentiated work

The Persona Engine, Dialogue Bank, Accent Coach, Impulse Engine, Scene Coach and the improv drills are the parts of this product that no competitor has at all. They sit behind a character sheet — the one thing five well-funded companies already do. The homepage argues on the axis where the product is weakest.

Every serious problem in this acquisition is a sequencing problem. Not one of them is a capability problem.

V — The Everweave question

You asked about becoming the next, much better Everweave. My answer is no, and here is the scoreboard.

Everweave is a solo AI Dungeon Master. It replaces the table. It has around 860,000 Android installs and a 2.12 / 5 rating that has fallen as it scaled. On iOS the written reviews sit at 3.6 against a tap-rating of 4.3 — the gap between people who clicked stars and people who typed sentences. Trustpilot: 2.8, with 34% one-star. The word predatory appears in reviews across three separate platforms. And in the hobby's own discourse it has close to zero presence except as a thing people wish did not exist.

Being “a better Everweave” means entering the paid-acquisition mobile-games market, pricing metered LLM calls against a hobby whose ceiling is about $6/month, and solving the long-term memory problem that nobody in the category has solved. You would be fighting for the player who cannot get to a table.

The Codex and the Vault serve the player who has one. That is not a smaller market wearing a brave face — it is the market where physical attendance is the retention mechanism, where the DM is already invested, and where you never have to simulate a person.

Everweave is still useful to us for exactly one reason: it is proof of demand for D&D on a phone at a scale nobody disputes. We inherit the proof and decline the war.

The incumbent clock

D&D Beyond's own roadmap (post #2223, 18 Aug 2026) commits to a player app “focused on in-person play” for early 2027. It lists zero roleplay features.

~6 mothe windowLong enough to own the category. Not long enough to spend on a rewrite.

The corpse in the room — and the test every plan on this page has to pass

Everweave is not the cautionary tale. Project Sigil is.

Hasbro built a D&D virtual tabletop with the IP, effectively unlimited capital, and 30 million registered D&D Beyond accounts pointed straight at it. It launched to the wider Beyond community in February 2025. In March 2025 roughly thirty people — about 90% of the Sigil team — were laid off, ending active development weeks after launch. It was sunset in October 2025. The servers go dark at the end of October 2026, and every piece of user-created Sigil content goes with them. Master-tier subscribers were compensated in store credit, not cash.

The stated reason, in the reporting: the “distinct monetization path” for the VTT never materialised. Rascal News' verdict was harsher and, I think, correct — it was “the video-game-ification of D&D,” built by leadership that “never quite seemed to grasp exactly what people love about playing tabletop games.”

So the test is not “is this a good app.” The test is: why does this survive what killed Sigil? Three reasons, and they are architectural, not aspirational. Sigil needed servers, 3D assets and a microtransaction economy to justify itself; we have no backend and a marginal cost per user of approximately zero. Sigil competed in the VTT category against four entrenched incumbents and one very expensive corpse; we are not a VTT and we do not want the screen to be the table. Sigil took the player's created content onto servers it then switched off; our storage is local-first and our shared log cannot be rewritten by us or anyone else. §VII, Law Three, is not a value statement. It is the post-mortem.

  • Feb 2025Sigil launches to the D&D Beyond community.
  • Mar 2025~30 laid off — ~90% of the team. Development ends.
  • Oct 2025Officially sunset.
  • Oct 2026Servers off. User content becomes inaccessible.
  • The lesson we are buyingThe dominant play mode, by WotC's own framing, is hybrid — about 60% (May 2023): digital tools used alongside a physical table, not replacing it. Hasbro spent the money on the other half of that sentence.
VI — The position

The quadrant is empty, and it is the one you already built

Serves the GM Serves the player Replaces the table Augments the table Foundry · Roll20 Project Sigil Everweave World Anvil · Kanka D&D Beyond (→ 2027) The Codex + The Vault

Four independent research passes · no occupant found · Sigil: servers off Oct 2026

Player-side · at the physical table · performance and roleplay · with memory between sessions

The state of the art in shipped player-roleplay support is a pay-what-you-want PDF with two ratings. That is not a gap in the market. That is the market not existing yet, next to a hobby with millions of players.

The demand is already loud and it is an anxiety market. Roughly 8 million cumulative YouTube views cluster around the same handful of words: shy, cringe, terrible at it, bad at roleplay. Today that demand monetises as ad revenue and $20 books. Nobody has made it monetise as software, because everyone who tried built a GM tool.

The Codex already contains the answer: Persona Engine, Dialogue Bank, Scene Response Bank, Accent Coach, Impulse Engine, Scene Coach, Conversation Drill, spaced-repetition flashcards. That is a performance coach for a nervous player, and it is already written.

The Vault supplies the other half nobody has: a shared, tamper-proof, audio-backed record of what actually happened, so the app can open on Friday knowing what your character said last Friday.

One honest caveat, because it cuts both ways. The in-person-versus-virtual split is not reliably measured. The last large-sample behavioural survey of it ran in April 2023 and nothing has replaced it — the Orr Group Industry Report, the most-cited dataset in the hobby, stopped publishing after Q4 2021 and was never revived. Anyone handing you a 2026 percentage is extrapolating. The strongest defensible statement is WotC's own: the dominant mode is hybrid, around 60%, digital alongside the table. I am betting the company on that sentence and I want you to know it is a sentence, not a spreadsheet.

VII — The thesis

The digital is an accent on the physical. Three laws, and I will hold the product to them.

Law one

The screen never gets the moment

Anything that makes a player look down while another human is talking is a defect, regardless of how good it is. The app's job is to end the look-down faster than it began. Every table-facing interaction gets a measured budget, and the budget is the acceptance criterion.

Law two

Preparation is where the technology is allowed to be ambitious

Drills, coaching, accent work, dialogue rehearsal, recall — all of it belongs to the week, not the table. At the table the product is a fast, quiet instrument. Between sessions it is a studio. That split already exists in the code as PLAY and PREP; it becomes the governing law of the company.

Law three

We never claim the work

Every death in this category came from touching the player's creations — AI Dungeon reading private stories, Kanka's entity cap, World Anvil “holding my work at ransom,” and now Sigil switching off the servers that hold it. Local-first storage and export-anything are not features here. They are the licence to operate, and the append-only Vault log is the architecture that proves it.

This audience learned the lesson in public. In January 2023 the OGL 1.1 draft tried to de-authorise a licence the hobby was built on, and within 22 days WotC reversed entirely and put the SRD under Creative Commons, irrevocably — then did it again in 2025 for the 2024 rules. Companies do not permanently surrender control over modest churn. That is the measure of how hard this audience will push back, and it is the reason our position on their data has to be architectural rather than promised.

And one rule about how we talk

We never lead with AI. Not once. The ENNIEs ban it, DriveThruRPG bans it, Kickstarter requires disclosure, Magic formally banned generative AI in final products after admitting “we made a mistake,” a seventeen-year WotC artist quit over it, and in June 2026 the Magic Arena developers won a union election in which guardrails on generative AI were a named grievance. The people who make this hobby have voted, repeatedly and on the record. We sell the outcome — you will be good at the table on Friday — and the machinery stays where machinery belongs: under the hood, offline-capable, optional, and never named in the pitch.

VIII — The proof

I stopped writing and built the thing the document promised

The Spell Slot Sigils have been specified since the beginning and have never existed. Here they are, working, to the letter of the spec — nine unique marks, level carried by structure and never by a numeral, ink that burns to ash and is rekindled by a rest.

Combat · spell slots · live Sigils

Tap a mark to expend it. Right-click or tap a spent mark to restore.

Toggle between the two. The shipped control is reproduced honestly from TurnRail.tsx:196-233 — same 44px target, same expend/restore gesture, same right-click-to-restore. Only the mark changes. That is the entire point: this is a drop-in, and the interface it replaces is onExpendSlot(level) / onRestoreSlot(level), unchanged.

The nine

Each mark is drawn from primitives — rings, lobes, spokes, points — under a seeded quill jitter, so no line is vector-perfect and no sigil is ever different twice. Level is structure: one stroke, a bilateral pair, a trefoil, a quartering, five lobes, a hex rosette, seven radials, a double ring, a mandala. Every mark above the first carries one deliberate off-axis flourish so nothing closes cleanly.

  • Forbidden list honouredNo Elder Futhark. No pentagram. Ink, never gold. currentColor throughout, so the state change is a colour transition and nothing else.
  • Motion budget honouredEmber flash 150ms · ink→ghost 200ms · wisp 400ms (decorative, non-blocking) · restore sweep 300ms · long rest 50ms stagger, 480ms ceremony.
  • Degrades correctlyprefers-reduced-motion removes the wisp, the ember and the sweep. The marks still render — they carry information, not decoration.
  • Cost to integrateOne file, no dependencies, identical props. ~4.5KB of geometry generated at runtime instead of nine commissioned SVGs.

The acceptance test was: a stranger describes the product to a friend by saying the spell slots are runes that burn to ash. You cannot pass that test with a document.

IX — What ships

One system, named for what it does to your Friday

Not a merge. A loop. The Codex owns the week and the table; the Vault owns what the table leaves behind; each one opens holding what the other put down.

  1. Release 1 · weeks 1–4 · “The Mark”

    Ship the sigils. Kill the debt that blocks everything after them.

    §VIII drops in. In the same release: cloudflared.exe out of history, the 18 backdrop-blur violations removed, the README rewritten to describe the design system that actually shipped, and schema versioning added to every stored character before a single migration is needed. Four weeks, no new surfaces, and the product finally looks like the only product that looks like it.

  2. Release 2 · weeks 5–8 · “Undo”

    The Vault's append-only log becomes the Codex's session spine.

    Every expend, restore, rest and roleplay beat writes a row. Undo is a compensating row, exactly as the Vault already does it — which means undo arrives, the session becomes replayable, and two tabs stop being a caveat. This is one architecture answering the highest-severity player-facing defect in the ledger and the integration gap in one move.

  3. Release 3 · weeks 9–12 · “Friday”

    The performance layer comes to the front of the product.

    Persona, Dialogue Bank, Accent Coach and the improv drills stop being a tab behind a character sheet and become the reason the app exists. The Codex opens on Friday already knowing what your character said last Friday, because the Vault told it. The character sheet becomes the supporting act it always should have been.

  4. Beyond · the studio

    Ash & Archive becomes the house, not a fifth project.

    The studio's landing page is built and Phases 2–5 never were. Good. They should not be — the DM Training Academy, the Toy Method and the world-building engine are the second product, sold to the person who already bought the first one. The Codex proved the training thesis works on a player. Ash & Archive sells the same thesis to the DM, on top of a codebase that already shipped, with a customer list that already exists. That is the sequence that turns 17/35 into a business; building the studio first is the sequence that scored 17/35.

XI — The number

About doubling the profits

I am going to be straight with you, because the brief asked for tangible results and not snake oil: you cannot double a profit that is currently zero. Both assets are free, neither has ever taken a payment, and any deck that draws you a 2× on this P&L is charting nothing. The real objective is not a multiple. It is the first one — and then the second one, which is the multiple.

Before the model, four numbers that should discipline it. I went looking for reasons this fails and I found them, so they go on the page.

$270–350MD&D · annualTriangulated by three independent analysts. Hasbro has never broken D&D out. It is 13–16% of a WotC segment whose growth is Magic, not D&D. This is a mid-hundreds-of-millions business, not a multi-billion one.
$5.99the actual ceilingD&D Beyond's top tier, monthly. Entry is $2.99. Books are not included at any level. The category leader, owned by Hasbro, with 30M accounts, cannot charge more than coffee money.
0premium contentOwned by the median Foundry user, per Foundry's own 2026 review. They paid $50 once and bought nothing ever again. This is the clearest willingness-to-pay signal in the hobby.
$0digital tools · crowdfundingNo TTRPG digital tool has ever raised $1M. The entire ≥$100K TTRPG crowdfunding market is ~$37–48M a year and it is books. Players fund books enthusiastically and software reluctantly.

Two structural moves, and they both fall out of that evidence

One: we sell to the player, not the GM. Every incumbent monetises the Dungeon Master. Roll20, Foundry and D&D Beyond's Master tier all share content downward to the group, which means the industry has collectively priced for roughly one payer per five-person table. We are the only product in the quadrant whose customer is the player. At half D&D Beyond's price, two paying players at a table beat one paying GM — and there are five of them.

Two: we charge once for the thing that costs nothing to run, and monthly only for the thing with a server bill. The Codex has no backend — it is a static PWA with localStorage, and it will keep working if we disappear. Charging rent for that would be dishonest and, per the Foundry evidence, commercially wrong. So the Codex is bought, once, like a book, because a book is what this audience has proven it will pay for. The Vault genuinely costs money to run — Supabase, audio storage, bandwidth — so the Vault is the only thing with a subscription, and it is optional, per table, and priced below everyone.

That is also the answer to the Sigil test. Sigil needed a monetisation path to justify a server-and-3D-asset cost base and never found one. Our cost base is approximately zero and our price is not trying to cover it. The same is true against Everweave: every metered LLM call they serve is a cost that scales with their success. Ours does not, because the AI layer stays optional, local-capable and bring-your-own-key. That is not a philosophical position. It is the margin.

Nobody who bought in year one pays again in year two, and that is the point: it is the shape of a business that can be trusted by an audience that has watched Sigil's servers get switched off and read the words “held my work at ransom.”

A model, not a forecast. 1,000 licences is a target I am choosing to be held to, not a projection from conversion data we do not have. The funnel exists — roughly 8 million cumulative views on content about being bad at roleplay — but views are not people, and I am not going to pretend a view-to-licence rate I cannot measure.

And the way this fails

Stated plainly so it is on the record before anyone spends a month on it. The willingness-to-pay evidence is genuinely bad. Software in this hobby monetises poorly, one-time-biased, and concentrated in the GM — and we are proposing to sell software, to the player, in a category where no digital tool has ever raised a million dollars. The $39 licence has to feel like buying a beautiful book, not subscribing to a tool, or it does not sell at all. If Release 1 and Release 2 ship and a hundred players will not pay $39, that is a real answer and we take it. What I will not do is discover that in month eighteen because nobody wrote the failure condition down in month one.

XII — Verdict

Buy it. Then finish the one thing you wrote down and never built.

The last evaluation gave this venture 17 out of 35 against a threshold of 20, and its stated reason was that Phases 2–5 had not been built and there would be nothing to sell for six to twelve months. That was a correct assessment of a landing page. It is not an assessment of what exists now.

What exists now is 107,584 lines of a product that survives a dead network, a poisoned cache and a full disk; that has been measured against 60 criteria by someone who left the failures red on purpose; that is installed on a phone and was used at a real table; and that contains — already written, already tested — the only serious answer anyone has built to the loudest unmet need in this hobby.

And beside it, a second product whose central architectural decision is a shared record that cannot be rewritten.

The gap between them is the company. Nobody is in that quadrant. The incumbent has told us in writing that it will not arrive until early 2027 and will bring no roleplay features when it does. The would-be giant in the adjacent space is at 2.12 stars and falling, and is teaching a very large audience that software should not be trusted with their table.

The single user comes first, and the single user is you. Release 1 is not a market play. It is four weeks that make your Friday better: the marks burn when you cast, undo exists, the cold start stops embarrassing you when the DM says your name. If that does not land for one player at one table, none of the rest of this is worth building — and if it does, the rest of it is mostly distribution.

That is what I would do. The sigils in §VIII are not a mockup of it. They are the first four weeks, started.

The bar is the red row with the evidence next to it.
